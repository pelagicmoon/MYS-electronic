筑波根に　雪かも降らる　否をかも　かなしき児ろが　布乾さるかも

Tsukuba peak, / the snow, falling? / or maybe not? / My dear, are you out there, / leaving fabric to dry?

In the Quechua language, when a person wants to say "I will communicate by radio," they would enunciate "willachimusayki radiopi." If we gloss this phrase in English, we would get something like "I will, in the radio, make it tell from me (I who am here) to you (you who are there)."

What I find most interesting about this expression is that the distance between the speaker (I) and listener (you) is established on two fronts.

First, by using the infix -mu-, the speaker places the act of communicating through the radio at the listener's end. The infix is used whenever alluding to something that happens away from the speaker's present location, such as in the phrase "willamusayki wasiypi, mana kaypichu," which in English would be something like "I'll tell you at my house, but not here." So (ignoring the other infix for now), "willamusayki radiopi" would convey something like "I will tell you in this virtual location called the 'radio,' where I am not physically located in the present moment."

However, the speaker must add a second infix, -chi-, to denote that not only are they alluding that this act of communicating will happen the virtual space of the radio, but indeed that they are 'making' this virtual space speak on behalf of them. This infix is used to describe somebody making somebody else carry out a task for them, such as in the command "mihuchiy alqochayta", which says something like "make my dog eat," or, "feed my dog." So (temporarily removing the -mu- infix), "willachisayki radiopi" would mean "I will make the radio tell."

Thus, though saying only one of the two infixes is very likely sufficient, the most precise expression uses both. It confirms that the radio, while resolving a space that already exists between the communicators (the -chi- infix) by relaying messages on their behalf, also makes a space between the communicators come into existence (the -mu- infix) by concretizing the need to measure its insurmountability with radio equipped against its insurmountability solely under human capability, e.g. the ability to walk over or the ability to shout very loudly. In other words, space both preexists and is contingent to the radio's entrance.

The dual nature of this technology, which resolves a distance while having to illuminate that distance, consists my reading of this Manyōshū poem. Phrase 3—
いなをかも — "or maybe not?"

—is the switch that illuminates the space between the poet and their referent. Then, can we point to the poem itself (i.e. the 'result' of the poet's performance you have just read) and call it the poet's 'technology' to their distant referent, just as the radio is for the speaker to their distant listener? I think not. By my judgment, the ingenuity of this poem comes from how it cuts in front of the audience's narcissistic question, which is roughly: "How can I expose this poem as the poet's technology for calling out toward us (and not anybody within the poem)?"

Notice how phrase 3 specifically modifies phrase 2:
雪かも降らる — "the snow, falling?"

The on-off mechanism created in phrase 3 ('maybe P is true, or not') is redirected at the veiling snow within the poem's life-world... even when it could easily have been let loose against the poem's speech act itself. An audience member could have easily assumed themself the direct addressee of the poem had this on-off mechanism applied to the poem itself—‘Maybe I am telling you this poem, or maybe I'm not.’ Now, though, the audience can only get a glimpse of a conversation that distinctly excludes them. ‘Maybe refracted through the snow I recognize "my dear" (phrase 5), or maybe the snow isn't falling in the first place'.

I tried to portray this exclusion in my track: at 0:29, right after the vocal track finishes phrase 2, a snow-like sound effect enters, but it stutters before fizzling out (~0:33) to make way for phrase 3. I wanted to divert the listener's attention away from the vague artifice of the track in its entirety and toward the blatant artifice of the snow within the track. The residue of this snow-like sound effect is repurposed after the vocal track completes the second half of the poem—
かなしき児ろが 布乾さるかも — "My dear, are you out there, leaving fabric to dry?"

—to make it clear that it is not the poet who turns the snow on or off inside the poem, but some actor within the internal logic of the poem the audience is disallowed from seeing.

To put it rather unelegantly, there is a 'god' within the poem whose sole purpose is to inhibit or permit communication between the poet and their "dear"; the supernaturalness of this internal 'god' is more interesting to think about than the external supernaturalness we as narcissistic readers try to muscle through by psychoanalyzing the poet. We are not the 'god' of this poem. Neither can we flatten its technology (its skeleton) to our instant of reading, because let’s say we’re the poet, and the cloth waving over there on the mountain will always be over there, and the haziness of the distance astride the snow will always be that kind of haziness.
