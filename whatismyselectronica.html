The Manyōshū (万葉集) is an eighth-century collection of Japanese poetry which traditionally covers over 4000 poems in twenty books.

C. Michael Chin, scholar of early Christianity, once wrote (Chin 485) that "the capacity to be surprised, even delighted, by the sheer unfamiliarity of the past is a fundamental skill for any historian," and that such a capacity necessarily deals in aisthesis. Elsewhere, Dipesh Chakrabarty, in his evaluation of how the category named "subaltern histories" operates, opened up this question (Chakrabarty 107): "Are there experiments of the past that cannot be captured by the methods of the discipline, or which at least show the limits of the discipline?" I want to front these two thoughts for a moment because one of the bigger ways I would like to engage with the Manyōshū is by figuring out what Orikuchi Shinobu (折口信夫) intended when he thought of the term 万葉びと (Manyō-bito... literally, "Manyō people"). Why does Orikuchi use the phrase 感情生活の記録 (kanjō-seikatsu no kiroku... literally, "Record of their feeling-lives") when he introduces Manyō-bito (Orikuchi 307) as an important part of his scholarly idiolect? How could kanjō-seikatsu ("feeling-life") operate in the same way aisthesis operates within a historian's techniques?

Zaumne, or Mateusz Olszewski, creates sound art that, according to his website, "incorporate carefully processed vocal samples and field recordings, often alongside tickling sounds which trigger the ASMR phenomenon." I am a big fan of his 2023 album Parfum. One of the reasons easier for me to explain is that the sound feels like the return of something that had been scattered to sea. Perhaps the body of water inspiring Freud's "oceanic feeling" more closely resembles a pore of sweat that our body once knew it was secreting but had no way of registering. The way Zaumne names himself a "researcher of human moods, atmospheres, internet culture and its sound content" (emphasis added) points to the same kind of research Chakrabarty describes when he, proceeding to identify what "minority" histories must do, sketches the texture of a "struggling, or even groping, for nonstatist forms of democracy that we cannot yet understand or envision completely" (Chakrabarty 107).

I've decided to undertake Zaumnesque research into the Manyōshū. This 'research' will proceed poem by poem ('original' texts will be taken from a 1926 2-volume edition of the Manyōshū published by 有朋堂 (Yūhōdō) unless noted otherwise), for which I will create:

  • a translation of the poem
  • a musical composition, whose lyrics will be the poem in Japanese, and
  • a short essay explaining some facets of the poem, its translation, and my musical composition.
