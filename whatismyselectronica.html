The Manyōshū (万葉集) is an eighth-century collection of Japanese poetry which traditionally covers over 4000 poems in twenty books.

C. Michael Chin, scholar of early Christianity, once wrote (Chin 485) that "the capacity to be surprised, even delighted, by the sheer unfamiliarity of the past is a fundamental skill for any historian," and that such a capacity necessarily deals in aisthesis. Elsewhere, Dipesh Chakrabarty, in his evaluation of how the category named "subaltern histories" operates, opens up this question (Chakrabarty 107): "Are there experiments of the past that cannot be captured by the methods of the discipline, or which at least show the limits of the discipline?" I want to front these two thoughts for a moment because one of the bigger ways I would like to engage with the Manyōshū is by figuring out what Orikuchi Shinobu (折口信夫) intended when he thought of the term 万葉びと (Manyō-bito... literally, "Manyō people"). Why does Orikuchi use the phrase 感情生活の記録 (kanjō-seikatsu no kiroku... literally, "Record of their feeling-lives") when he introduces Manyō-bito (Orikuchi 307) as an important part of his scholarly idiolect? How could kanjō-seikatsu ("feeling-life") operate in the same way aisthesis operates within a historian's techniques?
